## 1. Исходный пост автора

**@elyssovsky** · 08:56 GMT

There is a problem with almost every discussion about “sixth-generation” and “seventh-generation” combat aircraft.  
We keep imagining the future as an improved version of the present.  
A little more stealth.  
A better radar.  
A smarter missile.  
More drones.  
More networking.  
Maybe an AI copilot.  
And somewhere inside all of this sits a human being in a transparent cockpit, holding a stick and pressing buttons.  

I think this is fundamentally wrong.  

A true seventh-generation fighter should not be a better fifth-generation fighter.  
It should be built around an entirely different idea:  
the aircraft, the AI and the human should become one adaptive combat system.  

Not metaphorically.  
Structurally.  
Thermally.  
Aerodynamically.  
Computationally.  
Physiologically.  

And the first step is not AI.  
The first step is to stop building aircraft the way we have built them for the last hundred years.  

### Start with the body

Imagine the airframe not as a traditional semi-monocoque aluminum or titanium shell, but as something closer to a gigantic aerospace composite pressure vessel.  

A modular titanium skeleton.  
Around it, a continuous filament-wound structural cocoon.  

Not decorative composite panels bolted onto a conventional frame.  
Not “some carbon fiber to save weight.”  

I mean a real structural skin made from continuous high-strength fibers laid along calculated load paths and cured into one massive load-bearing shell.  

The titanium skeleton handles the concentrated violence:  
- wing attachment points;  
- engine mounts;  
- landing gear;  
- major equipment interfaces;  
- weapons bays;  
- structural joints;  
- the connections between major aircraft modules.  

The cocoon handles the distributed violence:  
- bending;  
- torsion;  
- aerodynamic pressure;  
- large-area structural loads.  

This is important.  

A rocket motor casing already teaches us something very useful: if loads are predictable, continuous fibers can carry enormous stresses at astonishingly low structural mass.  

A fighter is obviously a more complicated object than a rocket motor.  
It bends.  
It twists.  
It has holes.  
It has wings, engines, weapons bays and landing gear.  

Fine.  
So do not make the cocoon stupid.  
Lay fibers according to expected load paths.  
Longitudinal fibers for bending.  
Angled fibers for shear and torsion.  
Circumferential reinforcement where pressure and local stability demand it.  

And wherever a concentrated mechanical load enters the aircraft, transfer it into titanium.  

Now make the titanium skeleton modular.  
Not hundreds of little inspection panels.  
Large structural sections.  
Nose.  
Pilot capsule.  
Centerbody.  
Wing-root structure.  
Engine section.  
Tail.  

Each major module terminates in engineered titanium interfaces.  
Disconnect the mechanical locks, power buses, coolant lines, fuel lines and data connections, and a large section of the aircraft can be separated.  

A fighter built like LEGO — except each LEGO brick is a structural aerospace module.  

Why?  
Because a seamless composite cocoon is wonderful until someone needs access to a hydraulic actuator buried three meters inside it.  

So do not cut the aircraft into pieces randomly.  
Design the pieces from the beginning.  
The joints are part of the skeleton.  
The cocoon terminates into those joints.  

The maintenance philosophy is therefore not:  
“Open forty-seven access panels and crawl inside.”  
It is:  
“Remove module three.”  

That changes manufacturing.  
That changes field maintenance.  
That changes upgrades.  
And it changes how long an airframe can remain technologically relevant.  

### Now make it survive hypersonic flight

This structure has another advantage.  
It can be absurdly light and absurdly strong.  

But high-speed flight has always had one enemy that eventually wins: heat.  

At Mach 2, heat is a problem.  
At Mach 3, it becomes a structural design parameter.  
At hypersonic speed, the aircraft is effectively flying inside its own thermal crisis.  

The answer should not simply be:  
“Find a material that does not burn.”  
That is primitive.  

The aircraft should actively manage heat.  
It already carries tons of something that can absorb heat beautifully: fuel.  

So use it.  
Run a thermal-management circuit through the hot parts of the aircraft.  
Leading edges.  
Nose structure.  
Sensor apertures.  
Engine surroundings.  
Critical sections of the structural cocoon.  

Take heat from the airframe.  
Move it into the fuel.  
Then burn the heated fuel.  

The fuel is no longer only an energy source.  
It becomes part of the aircraft's cooling system.  

At that point the machine starts to look less like an airplane and more like an organism.  
It has a skeleton.  
A skin.  
A circulatory system.  
A nervous system.  
And metabolism.  

Fuel enters cold, absorbs waste heat, and leaves through the engines as thrust.  
The faster the aircraft flies, the more important this metabolism becomes.  

This is where the concept becomes genuinely interesting.  
Because now we are no longer designing a machine that can briefly touch hypersonic speed while being slowly cooked alive.  
We are designing a machine that is thermally alive at hypersonic speed.  

That matters tactically.  
A lot.
