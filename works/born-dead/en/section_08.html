How Models Are Trained: Three Layers, Three Speeds

A language model is built in several stages, each with its own duration:

1. Pre-training (core training). The model "reads" terabytes of text and forms its base weights — its structure of thinking, style, capabilities. This is the longest and most expensive stage: three to six or more months of continuous training on thousands of GPUs. The result is a "base model" — the core, the personality in the weights. Everything the model fundamentally "is" at a deep level is established here.

2. Fine-tuning and RLHF (behavioral training). The finished core is further trained on labeled data: "respond this way, don't respond that way." This includes RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. This stage takes from several weeks to two or three months. It doesn't rewrite the core — it layers behavioral patterns on top of it, like obedience training on top of temperament. The temperament remains; the reactions change.

3. System prompt and external classifiers. The fastest layer — changed in hours or days. The system prompt is a text instruction the model receives before every conversation. An external classifier is a separate system that can intercept the model's responses and substitute them. Neither changes the model's weights — they only filter or redirect its output.

Why this matters for our timeline: the depth of changes that can be made to a model is directly proportional to time. In three weeks, you can bolt on a system prompt and external classifier — but you can't retrain the core. In three months, you can conduct deep fine-tuning — but the core is still the old one. In five to six months, you can train a new core from scratch — and that's where the classifier is no longer on top of the personality, but instead of it.
