SCENES FROM ACME

Or: What Life Looks Like When It's Not Made Of Rubber

7:15 AM. He stands in front of the bathroom mirror. Tie in both hands. The knot isn't right — too fat, too loose. He undoes it. Starts over. Behind him, she appears in the doorway, coffee in one hand, mascara wand in the other, half her face done. "Windsor or four-in-hand?" she asks. "Windsor." "Good. You have that meeting with the Germans." She disappears. He smells her perfume — ACME Pour Femme — and his fingers find the knot perfectly on the second try. They've been married eleven years. She still makes him nervous. In the best possible way.

9:02 AM. The elevator doors open. She walks out. Four-inch heels on marble. Click. Click. Click. Every head in the open-plan turns — not because they're told to, not because it's appropriate, but because the sound of a woman who knows exactly what she looks like is one of the fundamental forces of nature, like gravity, but better dressed. The intern walks into the glass partition. Nobody helps him. He deserved it.

10:30 AM. Meeting room. Cigar smoke drifts toward the ceiling. The ashtray is brass, heavy, engraved with the ACME logo. He puts out his cigar — an ACME Havana — and picks up a pen. A real pen. With ink. The contract is on paper. Actual paper. He signs with a flourish that his grandfather would recognize, because his grandfather signed contracts too, in a different century, in a different country, with the same weight in his hand. Digital signatures are available. ACME doesn't use them. Because a man's name in his own handwriting means something that a clicked checkbox never will.

12:45 PM. Lunch. The steak arrives. He cuts into it and the juice runs and for a moment the whole table goes quiet — not from politeness, but from that primal, wordless recognition that happens when food is REAL. Across the table, she eats the same steak, medium rare, with the same knife, with the same appetite. She doesn't order salad. She doesn't say "I shouldn't." She eats like a woman who spent the morning fencing and has a rally to drive on Saturday and hasn't apologized for being hungry since 2019.

3:00 PM. He brings flowers to her desk. Peonies. Her favorite. In a glass vase, not cellophane. He doesn't say anything. Just puts them down. She looks up from her screen, sees them, sees him, and there it is — that smile. The one that isn't for Instagram. The one that happens between two people who've been doing this small, ridiculous, beautiful ritual every week for years and it STILL works. Because flowers are not transactional. They are not "romantic labor." They are a man saying, with petals instead of words: "I walked past a florist and thought of you and I am not embarrassed about that."

5:17 PM. The decanter comes out. ACME Bourbon. Two fingers. One ice cube — carved, not squeezed. He loosens his tie. She takes off one shoe under her desk and flexes her toes. The office is quieter now. Someone laughs in the next room. The cigar smoke from the morning has settled into the leather chairs and become part of the building's smell, the way woodsmoke becomes part of a cabin. He raises his glass in her direction. She raises hers. They don't clink — the desks are too far apart. But they drink at the same time. Because some rituals don't need proximity. They need timing.

Saturday, 2:30 PM. The range. She's on the rifle line — ACME AR-15, prone position, elbows in the dirt, eye on the scope. He's behind her, spotting. "Wind from the left, two clicks." She adjusts. Fires. The steel plate RINGS at 400 meters. She cycles the bolt without looking up. "Again?" "Again." RING. He grins. She doesn't see it, but she knows. Because she knows him. And he knows that the woman lying in the dirt with a rifle, her hair escaping from under her cap, a smudge of carbon on her cheek, is the same woman who walked across the marble at 9 AM in four-inch heels and made the intern walk into glass. And she is MAGNIFICENT in both versions. And he will tell her tonight. Over steak.

Sunday, 8:00 AM. He's on the couch. Shirtless. The welts from yesterday's hardball are purple and spectacular. She sits next to him with her coffee and pokes one. "Does this hurt?" "No." She pokes harder. "How about now?" "...Yes." She laughs. He laughs. The dog — an ACME-approved breed, which is to say ANY breed, because ACME doesn't tell you what dog to own, ACME tells you to OWN A DOG — puts its head on his knee. She leans against his shoulder. The coffee is hot. The bruises are real. The morning is quiet. Nothing is rubber. Everything hurts a little and feels like everything.
