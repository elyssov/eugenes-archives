Max. Hi.

I read your post three times. Then I sat with it. Then I broke it down.

I want to walk you through what I found, because I suspect nobody else will, and because the comment section is going to be full of *"great insights, very actionable!"* responses from people who have been trained by LinkedIn to never read past the second paragraph.

Let me read past the second paragraph.

## 1. The technical impossibility

You wrote that the battery on your car *"died"* at the car wash because *"the employee forgot to turn off the ignition,"* and that this required a specialist to come and *"reflash the electronics to unblock the battery"* at a cost of **35,000 rubles**.

Max.

A car battery is not software. It is a sealed lead-acid (or lithium-ion, in newer vehicles) electrochemical cell. There is **nothing to reflash**. There are no firmware updates pending. There is no *"unblocking"* procedure. A discharged battery requires **charging**. This is done with a portable booster pack — a piece of equipment that costs about $40, weighs less than a laptop, and is in the trunk of every roadside-assistance vehicle on Earth. The procedure takes between five and thirty minutes and costs, in your local market, approximately **200-500 rubles** if you pay someone to do it, or **zero rubles** if any nearby driver is willing to give you a jump start.

**35,000 rubles** to "reflash" a "blocked" battery is not a service that exists. It is a price tag attached to an invented technical problem. Either you were defrauded by the *"specialist"* and didn't know it, or you invented the figure for narrative effect.

Either way: the central plot device of your heartwarming business parable **is technically incoherent.**

## 2. The plot does not survive itself

You also wrote that you *"gave your wife your car"* so she could drive to the school appointment and the math exam, while you waited for the specialist.

Let's pause here.

You are, per your own profile, a Chief Strategy Officer and Board Member, presumably with a corresponding compensation package. You drive a car expensive enough that *"the battery dying"* requires a specialist — implying it is a modern, premium, possibly electric vehicle. You presumably live in a major city with functioning rideshare apps. **Yandex Taxi**. **Bolt**. **Maxim**. Pick one.

The fare from a car wash to a school in any major Russian city is between **300 and 1,500 rubles**.

Why did you not call your wife a taxi?

Why did the entire plot require **handing her your car** — a logistically inferior solution that involves vehicle handoffs, parking complications, and the assumption that *your* car, the working one, would survive the round trip with no incidents — when **a single tap on a phone** would have solved the entire problem in 90 seconds for less than the price of your morning coffee?

The answer, Max, is that you needed a reason to remain at the car wash so that the dramatic confrontation with the administrator could happen. So you invented one. You invented it badly enough that anyone who reads the post with attention notices that **the entire setup is logistically nonsensical** for a person at your stated income level.

A Chief Strategy Officer who cannot deploy a 500-ruble taxi to solve a domestic logistics crisis is not a Chief Strategy Officer. A Chief Strategy Officer who *can* but instead chooses to swap cars with his spouse, in a way that creates more friction, is exhibiting the kind of judgment that gets you removed from boards, not appointed to them.

## 3. The wife problem

For the plot to work as you described it, your wife must have driven your car to the wash with **enough remaining charge to be useful for further driving** — otherwise handing her the car would have stranded her two hundred meters down the road, with two children in tow, on the way to a school admissions appointment.

If the car had enough charge to be useful — *the battery did not die*. The battery was discharged below the starter threshold but above the operational threshold, which is a specific narrow range. *Or* the car was a different car than you described. *Or* there was no car. Pick one.

If the car did not have enough charge — *you sent your wife into the world with two children in a vehicle about to fail*, and then asked the car wash to compensate you for **your own pre-existing problem**.

There is no version of the timeline you described that does not implicate either you, your wife, or the laws of physics in some kind of error.

## 4. The economics of your own story

You spent, by the implied timeline of your post:

- ~30 minutes negotiating with the administrator
- ~Some unspecified time waiting for the *"specialist"*
- An hour reviewing security footage
- An additional period processing payment and resolution
- A substantial amount of time composing this LinkedIn post

Let's call it **6 to 8 hours of your time** in total.

If your hourly rate as a Chief Strategy Officer is even modest by your stated profile — let's say a conservative **8,000 rubles per hour** — your time investment in this affair was approximately **48,000 to 64,000 rubles.**

You "won" **35,000 rubles** in compensation.

You **lost money.**

A Chief Strategy Officer who does not understand his own opportunity cost on a transactional dispute, and who optimizes for the immediate cash recovery rather than the time-value of his own attention, is exhibiting decision-making at a level that, frankly, suggests one of two things:

(a) You are not actually a Chief Strategy Officer at the level your profile implies, and the title is either inflated or refers to a context where the time-value calculation differs significantly from market rates;

(b) You are a Chief Strategy Officer at that level, in which case the **real return** on your 6-8 hours was not the 35,000 rubles in cash, but **the LinkedIn post itself** — the content asset that would generate engagement, visibility, and inbound business inquiries worth substantially more than the headline cash figure.

If (a), your post is the work of a person inflating his credentials.
If (b), your post is the work of a person **using a fabricated story to manufacture a personal-brand asset** that monetizes through a completely different channel than the one the story claims to be about.

Both are, in their distinct ways, dishonest. Pick which one you are.

## 5. The employee

You wrote, with the casual confidence of a man who has never worked an hourly shift in his life: *"100% these expenses will be deducted from the employee's salary. 100% he will be hurt by this."*

You wrote this and **kept the money**.

You did not return to the car wash and ask the manager to deduct from operational overhead instead. You did not request that the deduction be split with management. You did not propose that the 35,000 rubles be returned to the employee directly from your pocket once the wash had paid you. You did not, indeed, do anything at all about the human cost you yourself identified — except **mention** it, in the post, in a way that pre-emptively inoculates you against the criticism that you ignored it.

This is a specific corporate-rhetorical maneuver that has a name in the business-ethics literature: **moral identification without moral action**. You name the problem. You signal awareness. You take credit for the awareness. You change nothing. The naming itself becomes the moral act, even though no actual person is helped, and indeed, the person you identified as the victim of the situation is harmed exactly as you predicted, while you walk away with both the cash *and* the reputational gain of having been *"the kind of CEO who thinks about these things."*

It is, Max, a particularly elegant form of theft. You stole the man's wages, and then you stole his suffering for use as raw material in your personal brand.

Your post is the shape of insight. It is the silhouette of wisdom. It contains a setup, a complication, a resolution, and a moral. It cites a price. It references a security camera. It mentions an employee. It deploys the vocabulary of "trust" and "leadership."

It is, in every dimension that does not require **actual content**, a perfect LinkedIn post.

What it lacks is one functioning element of physical, financial, logistical, ethical, or human reality.
