# SNOW

Hans slipped and went tumbling—cursing all the way—into the darkness of the deep, snow-choked ravine. Ravine was not quite the word. It was more of a gully, carved over the years by a narrow forest stream.

At the last instant, Feldwebel Thomas caught him by the scruff of his greatcoat. Even while falling, Thomas noted, Hans had not let go of the MG.

The yelling, however, had to stop.

Thomas hauled the Gefreiter closer and hissed into his ear:

“Quiet! They'll hear us!”

The soldier fell silent in terror.

It was shameful to admit, but Thomas understood that terror perfectly. They were the only survivors of what had been, until yesterday, a full-strength regiment. Yesterday, what had happened would have seemed impossible.

Today, the mere fact that he and Hans were alive felt like a miracle.

…

After the rout outside Kiev, the Russians had begun falling back, no longer resisting with the ferocity of the war's opening phase. Their immense losses in men and equipment were taking their toll. The Red Army's resources and reserves had been exhausted—if not entirely, then severely enough.

Thomas's regiment advanced in the second echelon. The Wehrmacht, exploiting its strategic advantage, had even managed a partial rotation: fresh, full-strength formations went forward while battered units were pulled back to refit.

The 75th Infantry Division, to which their regiment belonged, had taken up defensive positions after the capture of Kharkov and happily avoided the meat grinder of the operation against Moscow. The Soviets, however, were not content merely to have defended their ancient capital. Immediately after the New Year, they went over to the offensive.

So Thomas had been forced to leave a comfortable billet behind. He had even developed a pleasant arrangement with a charming Kharkov Frau who spoke German rather well and had gladly offered a room in her flat to a gallant soldier of the Reich—the man who had liberated her from Soviet tyranny.

At least, that was what she said.

Thomas, who at thirty-five liked to think he had learned something about life, suspected that the larger ration allowance had been her true inspiration. By winter, the city was experiencing the entirely predictable difficulties with food.

They abandoned their warm rooms and went out to repel the Soviets' suicidal attacks beneath the friendly fire of General Frost.

Once the main assaults in their sector had been beaten back, someone at headquarters was seized by the unhealthy idea of mounting a reconnaissance in force. Thomas's regiment was selected for the honour. After the punishment the Soviets had already received, no serious resistance was expected.

The only mystery was why anyone needed to chase Russians who were already retreating and broken.

Orders, however, were orders.

Eventually, they caught the Russians.

Or, more accurately, the Russians stopped and chose to fight.

There were not many of them left. Two or three hundred men against more than two thousand.

Yes, they had taken defensive positions. Yes, they met the advance with concentrated fire—as concentrated as their shortage of ammunition allowed. Yes, the regiment suffered casualties.

But one man does not make an army.

The exchange of fire lasted less than an hour. Then the Russians began to run out of cartridges.

Normal people usually surrendered at that point.

These men attacked.

Hand to hand.

It was not yet horror. Surprise, certainly. Bewilderment. Some men even lost their nerve as they watched the Red Army soldiers rise to their full height and charge across the fire-swept field of snow.

Naturally, not one of them reached the German line.

The horror came afterwards.

The horror wore the shape of a man.

He walked from the Russian positions into the killing cold wearing only an undershirt and trousers. He was pale, and every soldier could see the reason. His arms were spread as though in crucifixion, scored by the deep furrows of opened veins. Short icicles of frozen blood hung from the wounds.

The horror came attended by a roaring blizzard. It crawled at his heels, swallowing everything behind him beneath the white darkness of snow.

Even that howl could not drown his voice.

It was indifferent. Dead. Its measured syllables struck inside the skull.

Dead words in a dead tongue. The sound alone made bones ache and drove infernal pain through the teeth.

He was not reciting those words for the living.

And those for whom he recited them heard.

When the first paralysis passed, of course, men fired at him. The horror did not appear to notice the bullets.

Then the fallen began to move.

The bodies rose—terrible, mutilated by rifle and machine-gun fire, but still warm. They had no ammunition left for their weapons.

They no longer needed it.

The dead Russians rose to defend their land after the living could defend it no more.

The regiment wavered. Some men ran. Others fired their machine guns in desperate, hysterical sweeps across the advancing corpses.

A waste of ammunition, as it turned out.

The horror had no intention of allowing anyone to escape.

The blizzard upon his shoulders lunged forward. It covered the living in a winding-sheet of snow, blinded them with flakes sharp as razors, and killed them with cold that seemed to suck the soul from the body.

Then the dead emerged from the storm at arm's length.

There was no chance of survival.

Frozen hands closed around living throats. The risen moved no more slowly than living men. Unlike living men, they cared nothing for wounds.

Hans struck the corpse clutching Thomas with the butt of his MG 34. The heavy machine gun served admirably as a club. The blow shattered bone and crushed half the Red Army soldier's skull.

But an injury could not kill what was already dead.

It did, at least, buy them time.

Thomas ran, dragging Hans after him. The corpse with the ruined head found another target and did not follow.

Thomas's first thought was to break through to the vehicles, but there was no way to navigate in the frozen murk of the storm. In that icy hell of men thrashing blindly in terror, instinct was all they had. Thomas followed his. Hans struggled not to lose him.

Then, amid the chaos, came a sudden stillness.

And they saw the one who had begun the nightmare.

The man with the opened veins fixed Thomas with a gaze that seemed to turn the Feldwebel's blood into streams of liquid ice.

No one moved for several seconds.

Behind Thomas, Hans broke the silence.

“Lord God, our Saviour!”

The undead thing reached one bloodied hand towards Thomas's chest and drew a golden cigarette case from his breast pocket.

Hans remembered it. Thomas had taken it from some Russian woman. Apparently, it had belonged to her husband since nearly the time of the First World War.

It had been a reasonable thing to take. She had no further use for it.

They had burned the village.

The dead man closed his hand around the cigarette case. For a moment, he shut his eyes—eyes the colour of old ice. Then he looked at the soldiers again.

He did not need to speak. They read the sentence in his gaze.

But nothing happened.

The dead man merely walked past them and vanished into the storm as it rose once more.

In the last instant of clear air, Thomas saw the edge of the forest.

Their dash towards the trees was a dash towards life itself.

Fortunately, none of the risen dead followed. They were occupied, it seemed, with exterminating the soldiers who still remained alive.

Thomas and Hans had no intention of waiting to be noticed.

…

Hans saw the lights first.

The blizzard ended as suddenly as if someone had turned a switch.

One moment there was screaming snow and a cold like empty space draining the last strength from their bodies. Then it was over. The snow settled, and a little village appeared before them as though it had stepped out of a Christmas card.

At the worst possible moment, Thomas remembered his daughters.

He began to weep.

He had stopped believing that he would return home. That he would ever see them again.

The village shone with lights as though there were no war anywhere in the world. Gingerbread Ukrainian cottages beckoned with warmth. Smoke rose from their chimneys while the Russian winter gnawed through Thomas and Hans to the bone, drinking the last remnants of life, strength, movement and thought.

Thomas forced himself upright and stumbled after Hans, who was already running a little way ahead.

The houses were only a little way off.

A little way.

But the field would not end…
