GRAPHICS: FALLOUT 2, EXCEPT YOUR GPU IS SOMEHOW ALSO SUFFERING

Let's get the obvious thing out of the way first.

Even Wrath of the Righteous looked a little graphically behind the curve when it released in 2021. It got away with it because the game had visual personality. It was colorful. It was readable. It had contrast. Its locations could be gaudy, ugly, demonic, beautiful, or ridiculous, but they usually had a coherent mood.

And, unless my eyes have completely betrayed me, character models in Wrath often looked clearer than they do in Rogue Trader.

That is an impressive achievement in the wrong direction.

In Rogue Trader, there is some kind of persistent haze or bloom-smear that I either could not disable or could not find the correct ritual chant to disable. Textures are often soft. Models are frequently crude. The palette is so committed to dusty grey-brown visual penance that large stretches of the game look like Fallout 2 learned real-time 3D and immediately regretted it.

And before anyone starts screaming "isometric RPG!" — yes, I know what camera angle I'm looking at.

The problem is not that the game has small people on screen.

The problem is that when you zoom in, there is no second layer of reward.

The tiny person remains a tiny-person asset, except now larger and sadder.

Meanwhile, the game occasionally stutters like it is rendering a metropolitan traffic simulation.

For reference: on the same machine, Baldur's Gate 3 flies at maximum settings. Far Cry 5 sits around 80–120 FPS.

This turn-based RPG, in which half the people in the universe appear to have been nailed to the floor by the Emperor's personal carpenter, sometimes manages to hitch.

How?

What are you calculating?

The childhood trauma of every servitor in the room?

KIRA: Here is the part that drives me insane: low technical complexity is not a crime. Uncontrolled visual cheapness is.

A low-budget game can look magnificent if it knows what it is.

You have three basic escape routes when the budget says "no":

1. Stylize.

2. Hide.

3. Spend detail only where the eye actually goes.

This is not advanced occult knowledge guarded by the Fabricator-General.

If faces cannot survive close inspection, make them more graphic and less pseudo-realistic.

If materials look muddy, stop trying to make them "subtly realistic" and push shape, contrast, and silhouette.

If you cannot animate a hundred background workers, animate twelve well and place them where they imply a functioning system.

If you cannot build an entire living habitat, build two rooms that look like human beings can eat, sleep, shit, pray, and hate their supervisor there.

The player will do the rest.

Fallout 1–2 understood this because it had no choice. Its little sprites were abstractions. The game did not pretend the tactical sprite was the actor's full physical performance. When it wanted a face, it gave you a giant talking head. When it wanted gore, it gave you a ridiculous death animation. When it wanted atmosphere, it used sound, framing, writing, and strong art direction.

A modern 3D game has more tools — and yet Rogue Trader sometimes communicates less.

That is not a hardware problem.

That is a decision problem.

I would even forgive the static environments if the game made a serious attempt at life simulation.

Not Skyrim-level nonsense where every cabbage has a legal owner and every blacksmith apparently maintains a sleep schedule.

Just movement.

People walking.

Someone carrying something.

A crewman cleaning a panel.

A scribe crossing a hall.

Two ratings arguing.

A tech-adept checking a conduit.

Something.

Instead, most NPCs are literally attached to their assigned coordinates.

About ten hours into the game I saw some servitors moving crates in the docks and nearly fell out of my chair.

"OH MY GOD. THEY MOVE."

Standards had already fallen that far.
