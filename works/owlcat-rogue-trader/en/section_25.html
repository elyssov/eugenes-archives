COMBAT: I PICKED STORY MODE BECAUSE I WANTED A STORY

I chose Story difficulty.

Deliberately.

Because I wanted to experience the narrative and world without turning every cultist into a spreadsheet exam.

After roughly twenty hours, I was sick of fighting.

Not because it was too hard.

Because there was too much of it.

Fight.

Walk.

Fight.

Talk.

Fight.

Container.

Fight.

Someone says "Lord Captain."

Fight.

I do not want to spend half my life processing street trash through a turn-order machine.

I want to negotiate with aristocrats.

Investigate conspiracies.

Judge governors.

Manipulate Houses.

Read the room.

Threaten people with consequences bigger than my gun.

I am a Rogue Trader.

I have armed men for the armed-men problem.

KIRA: And this is where transplanting tabletop DNA too literally becomes a trap.

The FFG-era Rogue Trader tabletop RPG is not from the 1980s — the role-playing game was published in 2009, while the original Warhammer 40,000: Rogue Trader rulebook is the 1987 ancestor of 40K itself.

But the tabletop RPG inherits old-school assumptions:

Human GM.

Flexible adjudication.

A group that can skip a dull fight because everyone at the table agrees it is dull.

A GM who can say:

"Your armsmen handle the cultists; you reach the command chamber."

A computer cannot improvise that unless the designers explicitly build it.

So all the tabletop modifiers, stacks, talents, conditions, marks, exploits, action economy and crunchy fiddliness become mandatory processing.

And Owlcat loves crunch.

God bless them, they look at a system with two hundred knobs and ask where to install another forty.

That is wonderful when the character-building puzzle is the fantasy.

Pathfinder can support that because Pathfinder is partly a game about constructing a rules-engine monster and watching it eat the encounter.

Rogue Trader's fantasy should be different.

The weapon itself should carry tactical identity.

Bolter: brutal, loud, terrifying, suppressive.

Melta: close-range armor eraser.

Plasma: dangerous high-energy anti-armor tool.

Flamer: area denial and panic.

Lasgun: reliable, logistically elegant, ubiquitous.

Instead, like many crunchy CRPGs, the game sometimes makes the weapon feel like a carrier for a tax form:

"After applying Mark X to enemy Y, gain stack Z, provided ally Q has used ability R since the start of the previous round."

My beloved, I wanted to shoot a heretic.

Why am I doing quarterly accounting?
