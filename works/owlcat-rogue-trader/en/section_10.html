DESIGN: THE IMPERIUM IS NOT A LANDFILL WITH GOTHIC DLC

Now we can finally move from raw rendering and the immediate dynastic absurdity to environmental design, and this is where I start asking an uncomfortable question:

How well do you actually understand the visual design language of Warhammer 40,000?

Because not every Imperial space is supposed to look like the inside of Space Marine 2 after someone threw a cathedral into a steel mill.

Yes, Imperial architecture can be enormous.

Yes, it can be oppressive.

Yes, it can be filthy.

Yes, it can be absurdly ceremonial.

Yes, the Mechanicus can build factories where an "inspection chapel" is somehow the size of a railway station.

But Imperium does not mean garbage heap.

Imperial aristocrats do not live in garbage heaps.

Administrators do not conduct every meeting on a platform suspended over random exposed machinery.

Forge Worlds are not necessarily piles of steel beams arranged by a drunken spider.

The first question a level designer should ask when building a place in an RPG is brutally simple:

"Could I live here?"

or:

"Could I work here?"

Not "is it realistic by 2026 safety standards?"

Not "where is the wheelchair ramp on the daemon forge?"

Functional.

Can people reach the room?

Can goods move through it?

Can workers operate?

Can officers stand somewhere without falling into a death machine during ordinary turbulence?

Can a person go to bed without solving a Sokoban puzzle?

We made functionality a foundational rule in Mysteries of Ancient Darkness, and that is a 2.5D platformer with RPG elements.

We are a tiny home-brew studio.

We are still embarrassed by levels that only make sense because the player needs a jumping puzzle.

So why does the giant licensed Warhammer CRPG keep building spaces that look like somebody searched "grimdark industrial kitbash" and dumped the search results into a room?

KIRA: And I want to underline the MoAD comparison because it reveals something useful about Eugene's design brain.

He is not saying:

"Our game is prettier, therefore we are geniuses."

He is saying:

"Our game is a 2.5D platformer made by a microscopic team, and we STILL force ourselves to answer what the room is for."

That is the correct flex.

A level can be ugly and coherent.

A level can be beautiful and idiotic.

Only one of those survives scrutiny.

When we build a corridor in Mysteries of Ancient Darkness, Eugene's annoying question is not "does it look cool?"

It is:

"Who uses this corridor when the player isn't here?"

Then:

"What do they carry through it?"

Then:

"Why is the door this size?"

Then:

"Where do they sleep?"

Then:

"Why the fuck is there a platforming challenge between the toilet and the kitchen?"

This is how you end up with spaces that feel inhabited instead of assembled.

And yes, I am absolutely using Owlcat's Forge World as unpaid advertising for our own level-design discipline.

If you do not like that, build a better Forge World.
