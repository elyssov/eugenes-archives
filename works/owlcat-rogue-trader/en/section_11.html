THE GIANT GEARS UNDER THE BRIDGE

Take the ship's bridge.

There are giant rotating gears under parts of it.

Why?

No, seriously.

Why?

What do they drive?

What system requires huge exposed rotating gears directly underneath senior command personnel on a combat vessel that can violently shake under fire?

This is not a lower-deck work pit where disposable ratings are crawling through machinery.

This is the bridge.

High officers.

Command staff.

The people you would very much prefer not to pulp between decorative gear teeth when an enemy broadside rocks the ship.

Are the Mechanicus stupid?

No.

Superstitious? Absolutely.

Ritualized? Yes.

Cruel? Frequently.

Wasteful by our standards? Often.

But their machinery is not random.

A Forge World can take centuries to certify a technology because it is doctrinally constipated beyond human comprehension — and then produce that certified object in terrifying industrial quantity.

That requires process discipline.

You do not mass-produce cathedral-ships by throwing girders into a room until it looks Warhammery.

KIRA: This is the distinction I want tattooed onto every 40K environment artist:

Gothic does not mean irrational.

The Imperium is irrational at the institutional level.

It can pray over a machine for three hours before replacing a fuse.

It can execute the engineer who suggests a more efficient fuse because the new fuse lacks doctrinal provenance.

It can spend a century arguing whether a recovered STC fragment is holy.

Then, once the approved fuse exists, it will manufacture six billion of the little bastards and install them according to a maintenance liturgy so standardized that a half-lobotomized servitor can do it.

That is not chaos.

That is ritualized systems engineering.

The Mechanicus is frightening precisely because its religion is also an operating manual.

So when you show me a Forge World where access routes are random piles of beams and machinery seems to exist mainly as level geometry, I do not think "wow, grimdark."

I think:

"The level designer needed cover."

And that is one of the cruelest things you can do to worldbuilding: let combat geometry visibly dictate architecture.

A good combat space looks like a factory that happens to support a firefight.

A bad one looks like a firefight arena wearing a factory hat.
