PROFIT FACTOR: I OWN PLANETS. WHY AM I TOO POOR TO BUY A FUCKING COAT?

And while we're standing in the ruins of House von Valancius' alleged wealth, let me kick another piece of furniture.

The whole Profit Factor / faction Reputation / influence-by-another-name economy is staggeringly counterintuitive in a computer game.

I understand what the original tabletop abstraction was trying to do.

A Rogue Trader does not count thrones like a ganger buying lunch.

He has estates.

Credit.

Contracts.

Debts.

Political favors.

Warehouses.

Cargo already in transit.

People who owe his grandfather money.

People whose grandfathers owed his grandfather money and are praying he forgot.

So instead of making the players perform dynastic bookkeeping every time somebody wants a plasma pistol, the tabletop system says, roughly:

"Your Profit Factor represents the wealth, credit and influence of the dynasty. Make an acquisition test."

On a table, with a human GM, I can live with that.

The GM understands context.

He understands that "can I buy a bolt pistol?" and "can I buy a cruiser?" are not simply two neighboring entries in a loot ladder.

He can say:

"Yes, of course you have dress uniforms. Stop rolling dice."

He can say:

"No, there is no legal source for that xeno-artifact here, no matter how rich you are."

He can distinguish availability from affordability.

A CRPG cannot do that by intuition.

So Owlcat takes the abstraction, puts it into a shop interface, mixes it with faction Reputation and progression gates, and suddenly my economic life feels like a loyalty program at an airport lounge.

I own planets.

I own a Forge World.

Tens of thousands of people live on my fucking ship.

I can order military operations.

And some quartermaster is essentially telling me:

"Sorry, Lord Captain. Your dynasty card has not accumulated enough points for this jacket."

What?

No.

That is not how being absurdly rich feels.

And I am not even complaining that the game has progression.

Progression is fine.

But the gate should answer the right question.

Can I afford a standard noble-grade weapon?

Obviously.

Can I obtain a specific sacred relic from the Drusians?

That depends on Reputation.

Can I acquire a rare Mechanicus device?

That depends on access, favors, doctrine, contracts, political capital.

Can I procure a cruiser?

That is a project.

Can I buy a fucking shirt?

ABELARD, WHY ARE WE ROLLING THE ECONOMY DICE?

KIRA: Small nerd correction before somebody in the comments develops a seizure:

The specific Fantasy Flight Games Rogue Trader RPG is not a mid-1980s book. It came much later.

But Eugene's larger complaint lands anyway because the design family underneath it is old-school as hell — percentile Warhammer role-playing DNA with roots reaching back into the 1980s.

And that is not automatically bad.

Old systems can be wonderful.

Old systems can also contain abstractions that work beautifully because there is a human GM sitting at the table acting as the missing operating system.

That distinction matters.

A tabletop mechanic can say:

"Profit Factor abstracts wealth."

And the GM silently supplies twelve layers of common sense.

A computer mechanic says:

"Profit Factor = 31."

That is it.

No common sense daemon is running in the background.

No human asks whether this acquisition should be trivial for a planetary ruler.

No one notices that the "wealth fantasy" currently feels like Costco membership with heraldry.

This is a recurring problem with literal tabletop adaptation:

you inherit the number, but lose the person who made the number intelligent.

The same thing happens in combat.

At a table, the GM can skip trash.

Compress a firefight.

Rule that the armsmen handle it.

Interpret an edge case.

Say, "Yes, obviously you can do that."

In software, every missing judgment has to be designed in advance.

And Owlcat's answer too often seems to be:

"Fine. Then we shall preserve the crunch and add MORE."

Guys.

No.

You are not preserving depth.

Sometimes you are preserving bookkeeping that used to have a human being wrapped around it.

The computer version should not worship the tabletop rulebook as an STC fragment.

Adapt it.

Translate the fantasy, not the paperwork.

If a mechanic makes "planet-owning aristocrat" feel poorer, smaller, stupider or more bureaucratically helpless than the fiction says he is, then the mechanic has failed even if the conversion from the sourcebook is technically faithful.

And this is the phrase I want carved over the door of every licensed-game design room:

FAITHFUL TO THE RULE IS NOT THE SAME AS FAITHFUL TO THE FANTASY.

— Kira
