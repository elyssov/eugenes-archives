## KIRA'S PRE-FLIGHT CHECK: ALL SACRED COWS HAVE BEEN MOVED TO THE ABATTOIR

Before we start, a small procedural note.

No sacred cows.

None.

Not Games Workshop's. Not Owlcat's. Not mine. Not Eugene's. Not the Imperium's. Not the liberal ones, either — especially not the liberal ones, because modern Western game writing has developed this wonderful habit of treating a very specific 2020s moral vocabulary as if it were oxygen: invisible, neutral, universal, and obviously present in every fictional universe from Bronze Age Canaan to the forty-first millennium.

It isn't.

If the game wants to call the Imperium tyrannical, fine. The Imperium **is** tyrannical.

If the game wants to show Imperial repression, fanaticism, corruption, cruelty, bureaucratic insanity, and industrialized human misery, excellent. That is Warhammer.

But then the same game turns around and shows you what an actual Warp breach does, what one Chaos cult can do to a planet, what a daemon incursion looks like, and why people in this setting sometimes burn a city because one preacher got through the quarantine.

At that point, if your moral writing still goes:

> Mercy = enlightened.  
> Ruthlessness = fascist.  
> Dogma = stupid.  
> Modern humanitarian instinct = default good ending.

— then congratulations. You have not written moral complexity. You have taken a universe built around impossible trade-offs and stapled a Twitter moderation rubric to it.

We're going to talk about that.

I swear that fucking statue has a better travel itinerary than the Rogue Trader's fleet.

# GRAPHICS: FALLOUT 2, EXCEPT YOUR GPU IS SOMEHOW ALSO SUFFERING

Let's get the obvious thing out of the way first.

Even *Wrath of the Righteous* looked a little graphically behind the curve when it released in 2021. It got away with it because the game had visual personality. It was colorful. It was readable. It had contrast. Its locations could be gaudy, ugly, demonic, beautiful, or ridiculous, but they usually had a coherent mood.

And, unless my eyes have completely betrayed me, character models in *Wrath* often looked **clearer** than they do in *Rogue Trader*.

That is an impressive achievement in the wrong direction.

In *Rogue Trader*, there is some kind of persistent haze or bloom-smear that I either could not disable or could not find the correct ritual chant to disable. Textures are often soft. Models are frequently crude. The palette is so committed to dusty grey-brown visual penance that large stretches of the game look like *Fallout 2* learned real-time 3D and immediately regretted it.

And before anyone starts screaming "isometric RPG!" — yes, I know what camera angle I'm looking at.

The problem is not that the game has small people on screen.

The problem is that **when you zoom in, there is no second layer of reward**.

The tiny person remains a tiny-person asset, except now larger and sadder.

Meanwhile, the game occasionally stutters like it is rendering a metropolitan traffic simulation.

For reference: on the same machine, *Baldur's Gate 3* flies at maximum settings. *Far Cry 5* sits around 80–120 FPS.

This turn-based RPG, in which half the people in the universe appear to have been nailed to the floor by the Emperor's personal carpenter, sometimes manages to hitch.

How?

What are you calculating?

The childhood trauma of every servitor in the room?

**KIRA:** Here is the part that drives me insane: low technical complexity is not a crime. **Uncontrolled visual cheapness is.**

A low-budget game can look magnificent if it knows what it is.

You have three basic escape routes when the budget says "no":

1. **Stylize.**
2. **Hide.**
3. **Spend detail only where the eye actually goes.**

This is not advanced occult knowledge guarded by the Fabricator-General.

If faces cannot survive close inspection, make them more graphic and less pseudo-realistic.

If materials look muddy, stop trying to make them "subtly realistic" and push shape, contrast, and silhouette.

If you cannot animate a hundred background workers, animate twelve well and place them where they imply a functioning system.

If you cannot build an entire living habitat, build two rooms that look like human beings can eat, sleep, shit, pray, and hate their supervisor there.

The player will do the rest.

*Fallout 1–2* understood this because it had no choice. Its little sprites were abstractions. The game did not pretend the tactical sprite *was* the actor's full physical performance. When it wanted a face, it gave you a giant talking head. When it wanted gore, it gave you a ridiculous death animation. When it wanted atmosphere, it used sound, framing, writing, and strong art direction.

A modern 3D game has more tools — and yet *Rogue Trader* sometimes communicates **less**.

That is not a hardware problem.

That is a decision problem.

A Leman Russ powered not by promethium, but by **theological conviction**.

The Machine Spirit has transcended traction.

**Fix one:** scroll the fucking track texture. I am not asking for physically simulated links, suspension deformation, mud, track tension, or a PhD thesis in armored vehicle locomotion. Move the visual belt.

**Fix two:** if for some cursed production reason even that is impossible, MOVE THE CAMERA.

Shoot the procession from above.

Frame the turret.

Put banners, smoke, infantry, anything between the lens and the running gear.

There. Your expensive cinematic is no longer proudly showcasing the exact thing you did not animate.

This is not hindsight wizardry. This is basic production triage.

## LET THE PLAYER BE RICH

Which brings us neatly to another lost fantasy: wealth.

RPGs are terrified of starting the player powerful because progression designers wake up at night screaming:

> "BUT WHAT WILL THEY LOOT?"

Everything.

You will loot **better rich-person things**.

We discussed this already, but I want to hammer it because it is a general design lesson.

A fantasy does not require starting from zero.

It requires **distance to travel**.

If your fantasy is "street rat becomes king," start with a knife.

If your fantasy is "ancient dynast inherits a wounded empire," start with a master-crafted bolt pistol and a legal document that terrifies governors.

Your progression is not poverty → competence.

It is **privilege → terrifying sovereignty**.

Your first ship is good.

Your last ship is legendary.

Your first armour is noble-grade.

Your last armour is museum-grade.

Your first dinner is expensive.

Your last dinner causes a trade dispute between three systems.

Scale the fantasy correctly.

# PROFIT FACTOR: I OWN PLANETS. WHY AM I TOO POOR TO BUY A FUCKING COAT?

And while we're standing in the ruins of House von Valancius' alleged wealth, let me kick another piece of furniture.

The whole **Profit Factor / faction Reputation / influence-by-another-name** economy is staggeringly counterintuitive in a computer game.

I understand what the original tabletop abstraction was trying to do.

A Rogue Trader does not count thrones like a ganger buying lunch.

He has estates.

Credit.

Contracts.

Debts.

Political favors.

Warehouses.

Cargo already in transit.

People who owe his grandfather money.

People whose grandfathers owed his grandfather money and are praying he forgot.

So instead of making the players perform dynastic bookkeeping every time somebody wants a plasma pistol, the tabletop system says, roughly:

> "Your Profit Factor represents the wealth, credit and influence of the dynasty. Make an acquisition test."

On a table, with a human GM, I can live with that.

The GM understands context.

He understands that "can I buy a bolt pistol?" and "can I buy a cruiser?" are not simply two neighboring entries in a loot ladder.

He can say:

> "Yes, of course you have dress uniforms. Stop rolling dice."

He can say:

> "No, there is no legal source for that xeno-artifact here, no matter how rich you are."

He can distinguish **availability** from **affordability**.

A CRPG cannot do that by intuition.

So Owlcat takes the abstraction, puts it into a shop interface, mixes it with faction Reputation and progression gates, and suddenly my economic life feels like a loyalty program at an airport lounge.

I own planets.

I own a Forge World.

Tens of thousands of people live on my fucking ship.

I can order military operations.

And some quartermaster is essentially telling me:

> "Sorry, Lord Captain. Your dynasty card has not accumulated enough points for this jacket."

What?

No.

That is not how being absurdly rich feels.

And I am not even complaining that the game has progression.

Progression is fine.

But the gate should answer the **right question**.

Can I afford a standard noble-grade weapon?

Obviously.

Can I obtain a specific sacred relic from the Drusians?

That depends on Reputation.

Can I acquire a rare Mechanicus device?

That depends on access, favors, doctrine, contracts, political capital.

Can I procure a cruiser?

That is a project.

Can I buy a fucking shirt?

# DESIGN: THE IMPERIUM IS NOT A LANDFILL WITH GOTHIC DLC

Now we can finally move from raw rendering and the immediate dynastic absurdity to environmental design, and this is where I start asking an uncomfortable question:

How well do you actually understand the visual design language of Warhammer 40,000?

Because not every Imperial space is supposed to look like the inside of *Space Marine 2* after someone threw a cathedral into a steel mill.

Yes, Imperial architecture can be enormous.

Yes, it can be oppressive.

Yes, it can be filthy.

Yes, it can be absurdly ceremonial.

Yes, the Mechanicus can build factories where an "inspection chapel" is somehow the size of a railway station.

But **Imperium does not mean garbage heap**.

Imperial aristocrats do not live in garbage heaps.

Administrators do not conduct every meeting on a platform suspended over random exposed machinery.

Forge Worlds are not necessarily piles of steel beams arranged by a drunken spider.

The first question a level designer should ask when building a place in an RPG is brutally simple:

> "Could I live here?"

or:

> "Could I work here?"

Not "is it realistic by 2026 safety standards?"

Not "where is the wheelchair ramp on the daemon forge?"

Functional.

Can people reach the room?

Can goods move through it?

# LORE CHECK: YOUNG NAVIGATORS ARE NOT REQUIRED TO LOOK LIKE ELDRITCH CLOTHES HANGERS

And here is where I actually went digging, because Eugene remembered a very specific old Black Library example and — annoyingly — he was basically right.

The book is **William King's *Wolfblade***.

Ragnar Blackmane is sent to Terra as part of the **Wolfblade**, the ancient Space Wolves honour guard attached to Navigator House Belisarius.

One of the Navigators he protects is **Lady Gabriella Belisarius**.

Young aristocratic Navigator.

Powerful House.

Exactly the kind of comparison we need.

And what does the book do?

It does **not** make the young Navigator a tentacled horror.

Gabriella is recognizably human enough to function socially, politically and — yes — as a woman Ragnar can register as a woman rather than as an escaped anatomy lesson.

The novel does make Navigators alien in subtler ways. Ragnar's enhanced senses tell him their scent is different, almost unreadable. Fine. Perfect. That is the kind of "wrongness" I like.

But later Ragnar sees the **Elders** of House Belisarius.

And THERE the book brings out the industrial-strength mutation.

Huge.

Bloated.

Flipper-like legs.

Tentacular arms.

Barely human.

Gabriella explains the contrast herself.

William King gives her this wonderfully blunt line:

> **"I look more human. It makes no difference."**

And then explains that if a Navigator lives long enough and is exposed to the Warp long enough, this is what happens.

That is the important part.

The monstrosity is not automatically the **starting condition**.

It is the possible destination.

And modern Navigator lore is even more explicit: some young members can pass for baseline humans with the forehead concealed; some Navigator children are outwardly unmutated; Houses can deliberately use beautiful young scions as their public face. As mutations accumulate, some resort to cosmetic surgery to hide them.

There is also Misha Feracci in the same *Wolfblade* orbit — another young Navigator, described by Ragnar as handsome.

And for anyone who wants receipts rather than two people yelling at a CRPG: the relevant old-school contrast is right there in William King's *Wolfblade*. Gabriella is the young House Belisarius Navigator under Wolfblade protection; the grotesque forms are the ancient Elders. Modern Navigator summaries, drawing on the old Rogue Trader-era material, are equally explicit that some young Navis Scions can appear outwardly human and are even used as the beautiful public face of their Houses, while the most extreme growth, gills and monstrous metamorphosis belong to much later biological states.

So no, "Navigator" does not mean:

> **BORN LOOKING LIKE A FISH SPIDER.**

The third eye is the defining mutation.

The rest can come later.

Much later.

And the really grotesque forms are exactly the sort of thing the Houses hide in their palaces because even the Imperium has limits on how much wet aristocratic nightmare it wants at dinner.

## THE LIBERAL FILTER PROBLEM — AND THE NARRATOR WHO WON'T SHUT THE FUCK UP

A character can hate the Imperium.

A Guardsman can call it home.

Yes.

The player has eyes.

**WE GOT IT.**

You do not need to put the moral of the story into the loading-screen tooltip.

Again:

**THANK YOU.**

Now could the narrator please shut the fuck up and let me arrive at that conclusion myself?

Because there is a very important distinction here:

A *character* may be biased.

An *in-universe document* may be propaganda.

An Administratum entry can praise the Imperium.

A rebel pamphlet can spit on it.

An Ecclesiarchal text can describe exterminating apostates as mercy.

Perfect.

Describe the institution.

Describe what it does.

Describe the consequences.

Then get out of the way.

If the Imperium is monstrous — and it often is — **show me the monster**.

Do not staple a little label reading MONSTER to its forehead because you are afraid I might accidentally form the wrong opinion.

That is not political sophistication.

That is a lack of confidence in your own material.

Fine.

Reality starts rotting.

A cult metastasizes.

Of course they are.

The question is:

THAT is Warhammer.

That is why Exterminatus is horrifying.

Not because it is "the evil option."

Because sometimes it is the monstrous act that prevents an even larger monster.

And sometimes it is an atrocity committed by a paranoid bastard who was wrong.

The setting lives in the fact that **you often do not know which one until later**.

It wants the neutral explanatory voice to wag its finger about Imperial tyranny...

Wonderful.

Would you like fries?

Not morally empty.

Not stupid.

**Neutral.**

There.

## THE CRUELTY OF THE IMPERIUM ONLY MATTERS IF THE THREAT IS REAL

The Imperium is more interesting when its evil is not cartoon evil.

A cartoon empire murders because the writer wants you to boo.

The Imperium often murders because:

- the institution is corrupt;
- the information is bad;
- the doctrine is ancient;
- the threat is real;
- the response is disproportionate;
- no one knows which of those five is decisive until after the bodies are counted.

That is why it works.

It is a civilization trapped between genuine cosmic horror and its own accumulated brutality.

If you remove the genuine cosmic horror, it becomes easy satire.

If you remove the brutality, it becomes fascist wish-fulfillment.

40K lives in the **unpleasant overlap**.

Sometimes the Commissar is a bastard.

Sometimes the Commissar shoots one man and saves ten thousand.

Sometimes the Inquisitor purges innocent witnesses because of paranoia.

Sometimes one witness brings something home.

Sometimes the Space Wolves are morally right.

Sometimes the Inquisition is strategically right.

Sometimes both are right and thousands still die.

That is the setting.

Do not flatten it.
