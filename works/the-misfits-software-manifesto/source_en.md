We Want Our Games Back. Four games. Zero budget. First seats at the fire.

## Chapter 1: The Rubber Apple

There was a cartoon about a little mole who found a perfect city.

Everything was clean. Everything was safe. Everything was soft. The apple on the tree looked like an apple until someone tried to eat it.

It was rubber.

That is where the games industry has been going for years, and if you are reading this page, you already know it. You have tasted the rubber. You have bitten into a $70 game and felt your teeth bounce off nothing. You have watched a trailer that promised fire and received a product that was room temperature, focus-tested into oblivion, and approved by seventeen committees before a single player touched it.

Let us be specific, because specificity is what separates a manifesto from a tantrum.

Mass Effect Andromeda (2017). BioWare — the studio that gave us Liara, Miranda, Tali, Jack — shipped a game where every female human face looked like it was modeled on a potato. Not by accident. The real-life model for Sara Ryder was Jayde Rossi — a striking woman. Her in-game version looked nothing like her. Meanwhile, the male Ryder looked exactly like his model. Someone in the room decided that beautiful women in games were a problem to be solved. The fans noticed. The fans always notice. "Potato Face: The Game" became a meme. The game became a cautionary tale. BioWare Montreal was dissolved. The franchise never recovered.

Horizon Forbidden West (2022). Guerrilla Games took Aloy — a sharp, distinctive, interesting face — and softened her. Rounder cheeks. Wider jaw. Less striking. More... approved. The character who was fine in 2017 was apparently problematic in 2022. Not because the story demanded it. Because someone between the two games decided that a conventionally attractive female protagonist was a risk.

Sweet Baby Inc. (2018–present). A Montreal "narrative consultancy" whose services openly included "Sensitivity and Inclusivity Reading," "Risk and Opportunities Assessment," and "Cultural Consultation." They worked on Spider-Man 2, Alan Wake 2, Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League, and dozens of others. Their CEO, Kim Belair, told developers at GDC 2019 to terrify their marketing teams into hiring consultants like her. A Steam group tracking their involvement — "Sweet Baby Inc Detected" — hit 100,000 followers within weeks. Not because gamers hate diversity. Because gamers hate being managed. When a character's face is redesigned not because an artist had a vision but because a consultant filed a risk assessment, the apple is rubber and everyone can taste it.

Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League (2024). A live-service disaster built on a beloved franchise, shipped with battle passes, seasonal content, and writing that felt like it was generated by a committee of people who had never played Arkham Asylum. Sweet Baby Inc was credited. The game flopped catastrophically. Rocksteady — the studio that defined superhero gaming — was gutted.

Concord (2024). Sony's hero shooter. Reportedly $400 million in development. Shut down two weeks after launch. Two weeks. The character designs were so aggressively committee-approved that the internet couldn't tell if they were intentionally ugly or if the art team had lost a bet. The game became a verb: "to Concord" now means "to spend years and hundreds of millions making something nobody asked for because every focus group said it was fine."

Saints Row (2022). Volition took a franchise known for gleeful, self-aware, over-the-top absurdity and rebooted it as... a story about quirky roommates starting a criminal empire to pay off student loans. The humor was sanitized. The edge was filed off. The fans — the actual, paying, loyal fans who loved Saints Row for being Saints Row — were told they were the wrong audience. The game flopped. Volition closed.

Dustborn (2024). A road-trip adventure where the primary mechanic was using "cancel culture" as a superpower. Words as weapons — not in a metaphorical sense, but literally: you defeated enemies by calling them problematic. It shipped. It sold approximately twelve copies. It became the poster child for what happens when you build a game for an audience that does not play games.

Do you see the pattern?

It is not that these games were "too diverse." Alan Wake 2 was diverse and brilliant. Baldur's Gate 3 had every orientation under the sun and won Game of the Year. The problem was never representation. The problem was that representation became a substitute for quality, and consultants became a substitute for vision, and risk assessment became a substitute for courage.

The result: rubber. Beautiful packaging, focus-tested marketing, launch-day reviews from outlets that grade on ideology — and a product that bounces off your teeth when you try to bite it.

Meanwhile, on the other side of the market:

Baldur's Gate 3 (2023). Made by a Belgian studio of obsessives who spent six years building it — three of them in open Early Access — and refused to cut corners. Romanceable characters of every gender, including a shapeshifter and a vampire. Not because of a consultant — because the writers loved these characters and wanted players to love them too. Result: over 10 million copies, universal acclaim, Game of the Year from everywhere, and a playerbase that is still modding it two years later.

Elden Ring (2022). FromSoftware and Hidetaka Miyazaki. No accessibility consultants. No difficulty slider. No sensitivity readers. A game that says "this is what we made, deal with it" — and 25 million people dealt with it with joy.

Kingdom Come: Deliverance (2018). A Czech studio made a historically accurate medieval RPG set in Bohemia with no Black characters, because there were no Black people in 15th-century rural Bohemia. The press lost its mind. The game sold 6 million copies to people who wanted historical accuracy and got it.

Warhammer (everything). Games Workshop has been making absurdly violent, gloriously over-the-top, politically incorrect content for four decades. Space Marines are fascist supersoldiers. Orks are comic relief murder machines. The Imperium of Man is a theocratic nightmare. Nobody cares. Everyone buys the miniatures. The franchise is worth billions.

Helldivers 2 (2024). A game where you play as disposable soldiers in a satirical fascist regime, spreading "managed democracy" through superior firepower. The satire is so thick you could cut it with a combat knife. It sold millions. Nobody filed a sensitivity report.

The pattern is clear: games that trust their audience — that say "you are an adult, here is an adult experience, we will not apologize for it" — win. Games that treat their audience like a liability to be managed — lose.

We are tired of rubber.

We are making real apples.

This is not normal. This is not a metaphor. This is our Tuesday.

## Chapter 3: Adult Worlds, Not Rubber Worlds

Misfits makes adult games.

That does not mean porn. It does not mean shock for clicks. It does not mean a bucket of blood dumped on a weak scene so nobody notices the writing is dead.

It means our worlds have bodies.

Beauty, desire, fear, loyalty, cowardice, blood, smoke, faith, lust, exhaustion, guilt, tenderness, cruelty, humor, class, sex, history, myth, biology, and consequence are all allowed in the room.

If a woman in the story is beautiful, sexual, vulnerable, terrifying, rescued, rescuing, ruined, or dangerous, we will not hide it behind a safe gray mask. Alice Compton in MOAD starts as a beautiful woman in danger. Then the woman in danger becomes the danger. And the player mourns the beauty they lost — not because it was taken away by a consultant, but because the story earned its transformation.

If a man in the story is strong, masculine, protective, broken, noble, damned, a father, a soldier, a coward, or a monster, we will not apologize for his strength or his fall. Lord Compton is an arrogant imperial bastard who talks to Egyptian locals like furniture. He is also a devoted husband who will walk into a tomb full of things that should not exist to save his son. Both of those are true. Neither will be softened.

We are not fighting minorities, and we do not cast out of hatred.

Queer characters, different peoples, different classes, different bodies, and different fates exist in our games where they are born from the world, the era, geography, myth, war, family, and story. Not because a sticker is needed on the box. Not because someone forbade them to exist.

The world decides. The story decides. The character decides.

In Arcadia, fairies follow the logic of Arcadia. In ORPP, a squad's composition follows the logic of the Russian Empire, service, recruitment, secret societies, and strange exceptions — not a quota table. One early character in COLD IRON is Vanya — a Black Russian man, child of the aftershock of the 1980 Moscow Olympics, Russian to the marrow, furious when anyone tries to turn him into a category. He is not a category. He is a person, with a history, a temper, a flag in his bones, and a place in the world that produced him. That is the rule in one man: the world decides.

Drinking is allowed. Smoking is allowed when it hits the character nerve. Violence is allowed when the story earns it. Romance is allowed. Erotic tension is allowed. A makeover scene can cut to black and still do more work than a thousand explicit pixels. We are not making pornography. We are making games where adults behave like adults, which apparently is now a radical position.

Story first. Bodies matter. Beauty matters. Courage matters. Consequences matter.

## Chapter 5: The Four Fires

We are not pretending to have a finished studio machine. We are building one in public, with visible work, visible mistakes, and visible proof. The first slate has four fires.

### Fire One: Mystery of Ancient Darkness (MOAD)

Genre: Hand-painted occult pulp action-adventure with survival horror elements. Setting: Egypt, 1932. Tagline: The woman in danger becomes the danger.

Lord Eugene Compton — British aristocrat, war veteran, arrogant imperial bastard, devoted husband and father — arrives in Egypt with his wife Alice to find their kidnapped son, Alexander.

What follows is a descent into tombs, darkness, ancient powers, and a transformation that will cost Alice everything she is.

The game is built around asymmetric dual-protagonist gameplay. Eugene is the Empire's blunt instrument: military training, a modified Webley revolver with custom ammunition, the ability to read ancient inscriptions and intimidate anyone who does not like the color of his skin. Alice is his opposite: warm, empathetic, the one the locals actually talk to. You need both. His arrogance creates situations where only her kindness can open doors. Her vulnerability creates situations where only his gun can close them.

The core mechanic: every time Eugene dies, Alice can bring him back. The price is a piece of her humanity. Stage by stage — nine stages in total — she transforms into something ancient, powerful, and terrifying. The player watches the woman who bandaged a child's knee in Act One become something that makes the enemies run in Act Three. And they mourn. Because they caused it. Every resurrection was their choice.

The enemies are finite. Every bullet is a decision. Every fight is a tactical puzzle, not a button-mashing carnival. Six factions in conflict: the British expedition, the American tomb raiders, the Ahnenerbe (German occultists), the undead Pharaoh and his mummies, the Cult of the Ancient Gods, and the Ancient Gods themselves — who are not Lovecraftian tentacle monsters but projections from the Old Testament: Ophanim, wheels within wheels, covered in eyes, burning, rotating, wrong. When they appear — and they will — the art style itself breaks. The hand-painted world begins to rot. Walls grow eyes. Geometry stops making sense.

Hub-based structure with camp phases between missions. A hidden timer for Alexander's rescue that the player does not know about until mid-game. Milestone-based character progression — no XP grinding, every skill increase is earned through story. A camp economy where crafting ammunition costs sleep, and sleep deprivation costs combat effectiveness.

Status: Full design dossier (4,000+ lines). Thirteen-frame cinematic prologue with complete voice direction. Character designs, weapon lineages, faction conflict matrix, nine transformation stages, hub structure, camp economy — all documented. See the MOAD page with visual proof. Actively seeking team members.

### Fire Two: Tanks of Thea

Genre: Tactical narrative strategy. Setting: Generation ship, deep space. Humanity fleeing a dying Earth. Tagline: Both sides are right. Both sides are wrong. Every order costs lives.

A generation ship carries the last remnants of humanity toward a new world. A retrovirus called the Syndrome of Cain has begun transforming colonists into something post-human. Two factions — Vanguard and Concordia — are tearing the ship apart from within. Colonel Alexander Greyhawk is the last sane officer trying to hold the whole mess together.

Twelve player tanks across three design lineages. Five Vanguard classes named after Manhattan Project scientists — because when you are building weapons to save your species, you honor the tradition. Crew upgrade system. Cinematic storyboard sequences. Full lore bible.

The tank is not just a vehicle. It is a room, a coffin, a church, a workplace, a weapon, and a pressure cooker. Smoke in the tank. Bad jokes after battle. Drinks after a mission if anyone is still alive to pour them. A son can die "by the regulations," and that can be more terrifying than any explosion.

This is a war story about command, sacrifice, class, ideology, family, loyalty, and the biological horror of transformation. We need people who can make metal feel heavy and orders feel expensive.

Status: Complete world-building, faction design, tank lineages, protagonist arc. Twenty-four-frame cinematic storyboard. Seeking team members for prototyping.

### Fire Three: FAKEL / ORPP: Inferno

Genre: Text-based survival horror / tactical coordinator sim. Setting: Alternate Russian Empire. Paranormal military division. The love of your life is inside a zone that eats people. Tagline: A love story told through a military communicator during the end of the world.

You are a wounded intelligence analyst sitting in a tiltrotor aircraft outside an anomalous zone that has swallowed a city of 20,000 people. Inside the zone is your fiancée — the last survivor of her team. You coordinate her through a quantum communicator called Fakel-12.

The interface IS the game. It starts as a warm, white-and-blue messenger: colleagues joking about cafeteria food, an email from HR about workplace romance policies, a message from your general saying "one more mission and you're getting married, son." Then the zone activates, and the messenger becomes a dark military terminal with heartbeat monitors, drone feeds, and a nuclear cleansing button that is always, always available.

Ten drones for the entire game. A stress meter that determines whether your fiancée follows orders or panics. A personal corruption gauge — at 100%, she stops being human. Three communication tones: tactical, supportive, drill-sergeant. One evacuation capsule. Multiple candidates. Consequences unknown. Eight endings. One of them might be happy.

Text does more here than pixels. Voice, delay, silence, message timing, panic, and trust are the battlefield. A makeover scene can happen in the messenger — and the cut-to-black will be earned, because by that point you will have spent hours worrying about whether she is alive, and the relief of knowing she is will do more than any animation.

Status: Complete 1,500-line design document. Three interface prototypes built. Full ORPP universe lore documented across four published canonical stories. Ready for development.

### Fire Four: COLD IRON

Genre: Tactical horror, inspired by the brutal clarity of classic UFO: Enemy Unknown. Setting: Modern paranormal war in the ORPP universe. Tagline: Every bullet has a story. Every monster has a personnel file.

This is the fourth fire, and it is the one for the people who still remember what it felt like to lose a veteran soldier to a Chrysalid in 1994 and carry that grief for the rest of the mission.

Squads. Permadeath. Wounds by body zone. Ammunition that matters. Cover that matters. Panic that matters. Every bullet has a story, and some stories end with a squadmate bleeding out in a corridor because you miscounted your remaining rounds.

The setting is modern paranormal war: ORPP/AEGIS operational groups against demon incursions, fairy games, haunting zones, human transformations, Inferno events, things from deep water, and monsters that used to be people. Cold iron, silver, faith, psi coordination, telepathic squad links, field science, ugly bureaucracy, and impossible choices.

The tactical layer should feel clean, readable, and merciless — like the original X-COM, but without the UI that required a PhD to navigate. The strategic layer is ORPP-native: investigations, manifestation growth, research, squad rotation (Alpha/Beta/Gamma), casualties, cover-ups, and the knowledge that the monster in the next room may have a personnel file and a family who reported them missing.

This is not a nostalgia project. It is a war room with old rules sharpened for a new nightmare.

Status: ORPP universe fully developed. RURK tabletop system (the rules engine underlying COLD IRON) tested through live campaigns. Character concepts and tactical frameworks in development. Seeking game designers who know why the original X-COM still works.

## Chapter 6: How It Works (Practically)

Let us be honest about the uncomfortable part, because honesty is what separates us from rubber.

There is no salary yet.

There. We said it. No salary. No equity vesting schedule. No stock options. No signing bonus. No free lunches, because there is no office to eat lunch in.

What there is: a model that makes sense if you think about it for more than thirty seconds.

### The Fire Model

Every project is a fire. You sit at a fire by contributing accepted work to that project. When that project earns revenue — sales, licensing, crowdfunding, investor funding, whatever — the people who carried that fire share the revenue equally.

Not proportionally to hours logged. Not weighted by seniority. Not adjusted for title. Equally. Among the people who actually carried the fire.

### How It Actually Works Day-To-Day

You have a day job? Keep it. We are not asking you to quit. We are not a cult. We are a band, and bands rehearse in the evenings.

You are looking for work? Keep looking. We are doing the same thing. If you find a job and still want to come to rehearsal on weekends — your chair stays warm. If the job eats you alive and you disappear for three months — the chair goes cold, and when you come back, we talk about where you fit now.

You can dedicate twenty hours a week? Great. You can dedicate four? Also great, if those four hours produce something real. We do not track hours. We track output. We track whether the thing you said you would make actually gets made, and whether it is good.

### The Contribution Ledger

Kira maintains a contribution ledger. Not a timesheet. Not a surveillance system. A workshop memory: what was made, what was accepted, where someone was blocked, where someone disappeared. It is visible to everyone in the project. Phoebe audits it periodically to make sure Kira is not being too merciful (she tends to be).

The ledger is simple: if your name is on it with accepted work, you are in the contributor pool. If it has been cold for too long, we have a conversation. Not a firing — a conversation. Maybe life happened. Maybe the project shifted away from your skills. Maybe you need a different chair. We figure it out like adults, not like HR.

### Multi-Fire Participation

You can sit at more than one fire. If you are a sound designer and you can carry MOAD's atmospheric horror AND Tanks of Thea's metallic war soundscape — two chairs, two shares. A composer who scores all four games sits at all four fires and shares in all four.

The founder — Eugene — sits at all four fires by default because he carries IP, creative direction, organization, public face, producing, and shared risk across all projects. That is not a "boss tax." That is a person working at every fire. If a future project is fully delegated and he is not carrying it — that chair can shrink or disappear with the work.

The system does not know the word "director." It does not know the word "contractor." It only knows the fire you actually keep burning.

### No Boss Tax

This deserves its own line because it is unusual enough to be suspicious:

The founder does not take a special cut. No "founder's shares." No "creative director's premium." No "I was here first" surcharge. Equal shares among contributors, full stop.

Why? Because the moment the founder takes a bigger cut "for being the founder," the model becomes a company. And companies hire people. And people who are hired are employees. And employees need salaries. And we do not have salaries. So we do not have employees. We have partners. Partners share equally or they are not partners.

This is not generosity. It is arithmetic.

We are opening the first 6–9 public seats. Seats at the fire, not jobs at a desk.

Corporations have vacancies. We have chairs.

Current first-wave needs:

Game Developers (2–3 seats). People who can build playable systems, not just clean folders. You have shipped a mod. You have built a jam game. You have an arcade prototype that your friends actually played more than once. Show us. We do not care about your degree. We care about your demo.

2D Artists / Animators (2 seats). Hand-painted style. Frame-by-frame animation. Characters who breathe. Monsters who terrify. Backgrounds that were architecture before they were puzzles. If you can make a character feel heavy in twelve frames — we want you. If you can paint a tomb room that makes the viewer feel the dust — we need you.

Technical Artist / 3D / VFX (1 seat). Old Testament angel projections in MOAD. Tank models for Thea. Spacecraft for FAKEL. Anomalous zone effects. Clean geometry, good shaders, a feel for the uncanny. You do not need AAA experience. You need taste and the ability to make something that feels wrong in exactly the right way.

Sound Designer / Composer (1 seat). This is the hardest seat at the fire, because we have four projects and they all sound completely different. MOAD needs breath, aristocratic intimacy, infrasound, the pressure of something ancient in the next room. FAKEL needs communicator static, heartbeat monitors, the crackle of a dying radio, and silence — real silence, the kind that makes you hold your breath. COLD IRON needs weapons, monsters, squad radio chatter, panic, and the sound of a door you do not want to open. Tanks of Thea needs the tank to sound like a musical instrument of war — metal under stress, engines in vacuum, the hum of a generation ship dying around you. One person, four worlds. If that excites you instead of scaring you — you are ours.

Systems / Game Designer (1 seat). Someone who knows why UFO: Enemy Unknown still works thirty years later and can protect its brutal clarity without worshipping its ancient UI. Someone who can balance ammunition economy, hidden timers, faction enemy budgets, and milestone-based progression without making any of it feel like math homework. Someone who loves spreadsheets the way artists love brushes. If you have ever calculated optimal damage per bullet remaining at 3 AM because you could not sleep until the numbers felt right — call us immediately.

QA / Breaker (1 seat). Someone who can say "this is fake" before Eugene has to. Someone who plays a build and finds the moment where the game lies — where the collision does not match the art, where the enemy placement breaks the tension, where the difficulty curve promises challenge and delivers tedium. The person who makes everyone uncomfortable because they are always right.

Community / Marketing / Loudspeaker (1 seat). Someone who can show proof without turning it into LinkedIn paste. Someone who can write a tweet that makes a stranger click, watch a trailer that makes a cynic care, and manage a Discord without turning it into either a hugbox or a warzone. Loud is good. Loud and smart is better. Loud and smart and funny is the job.

### Bonus Points (Not Required, But We Will Notice)

Tabletop RPG experience. GM or player. If you have run a campaign, you understand pacing, player psychology, improvisation, and the art of "yes, and" at a gut level that no game design course teaches. If you have played in one of Eugene's campaigns — you are already family.

Modding experience. You have already proved you can work inside someone else's system and make it better. That is exactly the skill we need.

Bilingual Russian/English. Our projects are natively bilingual. Our lore draws heavily on Russian literature, military tradition, and paranormal mythology. A person who writes in both languages is worth two who write in one.

Voice acting. For MOAD's prologue (Lord and Lady Compton have very specific voices), for FAKEL's communicator system, for COLD IRON's squad radio. This is a later need — but if you have the skill, tell us now.

We are not going to pitch you a hockey stick revenue graph on a napkin.

We are going to tell you the truth.

There is a massive, vocal, spending-ready audience of gamers who are starving. They have money. They have hardware. They have time. We are feeding that hunger. Four projects, four genres, four fires. Each one built on a principle the mainstream has abandoned: trust the audience.

What we offer you is not a safe bet. We are not safe. But we are something rarer than safe: we are specific. We know exactly what we are making, exactly who it is for, and exactly why nobody else is making it.

If that interests you, reach out. We will show you the proof, the documents, the team, and the plan.

No rubber. No paste. Real apples.
