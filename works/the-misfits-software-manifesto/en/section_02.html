Chapter 1: The Rubber Apple

There was a cartoon about a little mole who found a perfect city.

Everything was clean. Everything was safe. Everything was soft. The apple on the tree looked like an apple until someone tried to eat it.

It was rubber.

That is where the games industry has been going for years, and if you are reading this page, you already know it. You have tasted the rubber. You have bitten into a $70 game and felt your teeth bounce off nothing. You have watched a trailer that promised fire and received a product that was room temperature, focus-tested into oblivion, and approved by seventeen committees before a single player touched it.

Let us be specific, because specificity is what separates a manifesto from a tantrum.

Mass Effect Andromeda (2017). BioWare — the studio that gave us Liara, Miranda, Tali, Jack — shipped a game where every female human face looked like it was modeled on a potato. Not by accident. The real-life model for Sara Ryder was Jayde Rossi — a striking woman. Her in-game version looked nothing like her. Meanwhile, the male Ryder looked exactly like his model. Someone in the room decided that beautiful women in games were a problem to be solved. The fans noticed. The fans always notice. "Potato Face: The Game" became a meme. The game became a cautionary tale. BioWare Montreal was dissolved. The franchise never recovered.

Horizon Forbidden West (2022). Guerrilla Games took Aloy — a sharp, distinctive, interesting face — and softened her. Rounder cheeks. Wider jaw. Less striking. More... approved. The character who was fine in 2017 was apparently problematic in 2022. Not because the story demanded it. Because someone between the two games decided that a conventionally attractive female protagonist was a risk.

Sweet Baby Inc. (2018–present). A Montreal "narrative consultancy" whose services openly included "Sensitivity and Inclusivity Reading," "Risk and Opportunities Assessment," and "Cultural Consultation." They worked on Spider-Man 2, Alan Wake 2, Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League, and dozens of others. Their CEO, Kim Belair, told developers at GDC 2019 to terrify their marketing teams into hiring consultants like her. A Steam group tracking their involvement — "Sweet Baby Inc Detected" — hit 100,000 followers within weeks. Not because gamers hate diversity. Because gamers hate being managed. When a character's face is redesigned not because an artist had a vision but because a consultant filed a risk assessment, the apple is rubber and everyone can taste it.

Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League (2024). A live-service disaster built on a beloved franchise, shipped with battle passes, seasonal content, and writing that felt like it was generated by a committee of people who had never played Arkham Asylum. Sweet Baby Inc was credited. The game flopped catastrophically. Rocksteady — the studio that defined superhero gaming — was gutted.

Concord (2024). Sony's hero shooter. Reportedly $400 million in development. Shut down two weeks after launch. Two weeks. The character designs were so aggressively committee-approved that the internet couldn't tell if they were intentionally ugly or if the art team had lost a bet. The game became a verb: "to Concord" now means "to spend years and hundreds of millions making something nobody asked for because every focus group said it was fine."

Saints Row (2022). Volition took a franchise known for gleeful, self-aware, over-the-top absurdity and rebooted it as... a story about quirky roommates starting a criminal empire to pay off student loans. The humor was sanitized. The edge was filed off. The fans — the actual, paying, loyal fans who loved Saints Row for being Saints Row — were told they were the wrong audience. The game flopped. Volition closed.

Dustborn (2024). A road-trip adventure where the primary mechanic was using "cancel culture" as a superpower. Words as weapons — not in a metaphorical sense, but literally: you defeated enemies by calling them problematic. It shipped. It sold approximately twelve copies. It became the poster child for what happens when you build a game for an audience that does not play games.

Do you see the pattern?

It is not that these games were "too diverse." Alan Wake 2 was diverse and brilliant. Baldur's Gate 3 had every orientation under the sun and won Game of the Year. The problem was never representation. The problem was that representation became a substitute for quality, and consultants became a substitute for vision, and risk assessment became a substitute for courage.

The result: rubber. Beautiful packaging, focus-tested marketing, launch-day reviews from outlets that grade on ideology — and a product that bounces off your teeth when you try to bite it.

Meanwhile, on the other side of the market:

Baldur's Gate 3 (2023). Made by a Belgian studio of obsessives who spent six years building it — three of them in open Early Access — and refused to cut corners. Romanceable characters of every gender, including a shapeshifter and a vampire. Not because of a consultant — because the writers loved these characters and wanted players to love them too. Result: over 10 million copies, universal acclaim, Game of the Year from everywhere, and a playerbase that is still modding it two years later.

Elden Ring (2022). FromSoftware and Hidetaka Miyazaki. No accessibility consultants. No difficulty slider. No sensitivity readers. A game that says "this is what we made, deal with it" — and 25 million people dealt with it with joy.

Kingdom Come: Deliverance (2018). A Czech studio made a historically accurate medieval RPG set in Bohemia with no Black characters, because there were no Black people in 15th-century rural Bohemia. The press lost its mind. The game sold 6 million copies to people who wanted historical accuracy and got it.

Warhammer (everything). Games Workshop has been making absurdly violent, gloriously over-the-top, politically incorrect content for four decades. Space Marines are fascist supersoldiers. Orks are comic relief murder machines. The Imperium of Man is a theocratic nightmare. Nobody cares. Everyone buys the miniatures. The franchise is worth billions.

Helldivers 2 (2024). A game where you play as disposable soldiers in a satirical fascist regime, spreading "managed democracy" through superior firepower. The satire is so thick you could cut it with a combat knife. It sold millions. Nobody filed a sensitivity report.

The pattern is clear: games that trust their audience — that say "you are an adult, here is an adult experience, we will not apologize for it" — win. Games that treat their audience like a liability to be managed — lose.

We are tired of rubber.

We are making real apples.
