Chapter 5: The Four Fires

We are not pretending to have a finished studio machine. We are building one in public, with visible work, visible mistakes, and visible proof. The first slate has four fires.

Fire One: Mystery of Ancient Darkness (MOAD)

Genre: Hand-painted occult pulp action-adventure with survival horror elements. Setting: Egypt, 1932. Tagline: The woman in danger becomes the danger.

Lord Eugene Compton — British aristocrat, war veteran, arrogant imperial bastard, devoted husband and father — arrives in Egypt with his wife Alice to find their kidnapped son, Alexander.

What follows is a descent into tombs, darkness, ancient powers, and a transformation that will cost Alice everything she is.

The game is built around asymmetric dual-protagonist gameplay. Eugene is the Empire's blunt instrument: military training, a modified Webley revolver with custom ammunition, the ability to read ancient inscriptions and intimidate anyone who does not like the color of his skin. Alice is his opposite: warm, empathetic, the one the locals actually talk to. You need both. His arrogance creates situations where only her kindness can open doors. Her vulnerability creates situations where only his gun can close them.

The core mechanic: every time Eugene dies, Alice can bring him back. The price is a piece of her humanity. Stage by stage — nine stages in total — she transforms into something ancient, powerful, and terrifying. The player watches the woman who bandaged a child's knee in Act One become something that makes the enemies run in Act Three. And they mourn. Because they caused it. Every resurrection was their choice.

The enemies are finite. Every bullet is a decision. Every fight is a tactical puzzle, not a button-mashing carnival. Six factions in conflict: the British expedition, the American tomb raiders, the Ahnenerbe (German occultists), the undead Pharaoh and his mummies, the Cult of the Ancient Gods, and the Ancient Gods themselves — who are not Lovecraftian tentacle monsters but projections from the Old Testament: Ophanim, wheels within wheels, covered in eyes, burning, rotating, wrong. When they appear — and they will — the art style itself breaks. The hand-painted world begins to rot. Walls grow eyes. Geometry stops making sense.

Hub-based structure with camp phases between missions. A hidden timer for Alexander's rescue that the player does not know about until mid-game. Milestone-based character progression — no XP grinding, every skill increase is earned through story. A camp economy where crafting ammunition costs sleep, and sleep deprivation costs combat effectiveness.

Status: Full design dossier (4,000+ lines). Thirteen-frame cinematic prologue with complete voice direction. Character designs, weapon lineages, faction conflict matrix, nine transformation stages, hub structure, camp economy — all documented. See the MOAD page with visual proof. Actively seeking team members.

Fire Two: Tanks of Thea

Genre: Tactical narrative strategy. Setting: Generation ship, deep space. Humanity fleeing a dying Earth. Tagline: Both sides are right. Both sides are wrong. Every order costs lives.

A generation ship carries the last remnants of humanity toward a new world. A retrovirus called the Syndrome of Cain has begun transforming colonists into something post-human. Two factions — Vanguard and Concordia — are tearing the ship apart from within. Colonel Alexander Greyhawk is the last sane officer trying to hold the whole mess together.

Twelve player tanks across three design lineages. Five Vanguard classes named after Manhattan Project scientists — because when you are building weapons to save your species, you honor the tradition. Crew upgrade system. Cinematic storyboard sequences. Full lore bible.

The tank is not just a vehicle. It is a room, a coffin, a church, a workplace, a weapon, and a pressure cooker. Smoke in the tank. Bad jokes after battle. Drinks after a mission if anyone is still alive to pour them. A son can die "by the regulations," and that can be more terrifying than any explosion.

This is a war story about command, sacrifice, class, ideology, family, loyalty, and the biological horror of transformation. We need people who can make metal feel heavy and orders feel expensive.

Status: Complete world-building, faction design, tank lineages, protagonist arc. Twenty-four-frame cinematic storyboard. Seeking team members for prototyping.

Fire Three: FAKEL / ORPP: Inferno

Genre: Text-based survival horror / tactical coordinator sim. Setting: Alternate Russian Empire. Paranormal military division. The love of your life is inside a zone that eats people. Tagline: A love story told through a military communicator during the end of the world.

You are a wounded intelligence analyst sitting in a tiltrotor aircraft outside an anomalous zone that has swallowed a city of 20,000 people. Inside the zone is your fiancée — the last survivor of her team. You coordinate her through a quantum communicator called Fakel-12.

The interface IS the game. It starts as a warm, white-and-blue messenger: colleagues joking about cafeteria food, an email from HR about workplace romance policies, a message from your general saying "one more mission and you're getting married, son." Then the zone activates, and the messenger becomes a dark military terminal with heartbeat monitors, drone feeds, and a nuclear cleansing button that is always, always available.

Ten drones for the entire game. A stress meter that determines whether your fiancée follows orders or panics. A personal corruption gauge — at 100%, she stops being human. Three communication tones: tactical, supportive, drill-sergeant. One evacuation capsule. Multiple candidates. Consequences unknown. Eight endings. One of them might be happy.

Text does more here than pixels. Voice, delay, silence, message timing, panic, and trust are the battlefield. A makeover scene can happen in the messenger — and the cut-to-black will be earned, because by that point you will have spent hours worrying about whether she is alive, and the relief of knowing she is will do more than any animation.

Status: Complete 1,500-line design document. Three interface prototypes built. Full ORPP universe lore documented across four published canonical stories. Ready for development.

Fire Four: COLD IRON

Genre: Tactical horror, inspired by the brutal clarity of classic UFO: Enemy Unknown. Setting: Modern paranormal war in the ORPP universe. Tagline: Every bullet has a story. Every monster has a personnel file.

This is the fourth fire, and it is the one for the people who still remember what it felt like to lose a veteran soldier to a Chrysalid in 1994 and carry that grief for the rest of the mission.

Squads. Permadeath. Wounds by body zone. Ammunition that matters. Cover that matters. Panic that matters. Every bullet has a story, and some stories end with a squadmate bleeding out in a corridor because you miscounted your remaining rounds.

The setting is modern paranormal war: ORPP/AEGIS operational groups against demon incursions, fairy games, haunting zones, human transformations, Inferno events, things from deep water, and monsters that used to be people. Cold iron, silver, faith, psi coordination, telepathic squad links, field science, ugly bureaucracy, and impossible choices.

The tactical layer should feel clean, readable, and merciless — like the original X-COM, but without the UI that required a PhD to navigate. The strategic layer is ORPP-native: investigations, manifestation growth, research, squad rotation (Alpha/Beta/Gamma), casualties, cover-ups, and the knowledge that the monster in the next room may have a personnel file and a family who reported them missing.

This is not a nostalgia project. It is a war room with old rules sharpened for a new nightmare.

Status: ORPP universe fully developed. RURK tabletop system (the rules engine underlying COLD IRON) tested through live campaigns. Character concepts and tactical frameworks in development. Seeking game designers who know why the original X-COM still works.
