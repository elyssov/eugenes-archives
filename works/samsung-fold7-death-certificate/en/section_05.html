"But She Dropped It"

Yes. The phone had been dropped.

Let me say that openly, because I know exactly where Samsung's warranty lawyers start licking their teeth.

The phone had been dropped. More than once. In a case.

As Alena herself put it: "Who manages to live six months with a phone and never drop it once? Give me a masterclass!"

And here's the apparently shocking revelation for the corporate priests of "premium care": real phones fall.

People have hands. Floors exist. Gravity is not an obscure edge case. It comes preinstalled on Earth and cannot be disabled in settings.

A normal smartphone is expected to survive normal human life. Not tank combat. Not underwater welding. Not being thrown from a balcony into a rock quarry. Normal life.

Especially if it costs like a small vehicle.

Especially if it's sold as a premium product.

Especially if it's carried in a protective case.

My wife is about 160 cm tall. In a hat. In a jump.

This wasn't a phone repeatedly dropped from a crane by an angry longshoreman. This was a premium device used by a normal person in the real world, inside a case, for less than half a year.

Here's the thing: Alena dropped ALL her previous phones. Every single one. Including a cheap Chinese Alldocube tablet that cost a fraction of the Fold. Not one of them has a single crack. Not one of them developed mysterious screen bleeding. Not one of them needed a repair bill worth a month's rent.

And yet the Samsung — the premium flagship, the one that was ALWAYS in a protective case — now "doesn't have a single intact spot on it," as she put it.

And yet the aluminum frame looks like someone tested it against meteorite gravel.

The chips on the corners aren't elegant little traces of use. They look like craters. They look like the frame is made of decorative metal-flavored pastry. Samsung can call its materials whatever heroic marketing name it likes — "Armor Aluminum," probably — but the photographs show an uncomfortable reality: the corners look like soft metal losing an argument with existence.

Look closely at the damage. The metal isn't just chipped — it's porous. The exposed surface looks like a sponge. Full of voids. Full of cavities. This isn't how quality aluminum behaves. Quality aluminum scratches. Quality aluminum dents. It doesn't crumble like low-grade casting with internal defects.

Either Samsung's "Advanced Armor Aluminum with +10% strength" is a marketing lie, or this particular unit has material defects that should have been caught in quality control. Either way: this is what a $2000 flagship looks like after normal use in a protective case.

If a premium device that costs like a Honda ADV, lives in a case, and is used by a normal human being ends up looking like lunar terrain, the question isn't "was it dropped?"

The question is: what exactly is this premium frame made of, and why does it behave like a luxury product only in the price list?
