III. INTERLUDE: WHAT HAPPENED TO LUKE SKYWALKER

Stay with me.

In 2012 the Walt Disney Company purchased Lucasfilm for four billion dollars. Four billion is, in case you have lost track of what a billion is in the era of trillion-dollar central bank interventions, an actual significant amount of money, even for Disney. They bought Lucasfilm for the express purpose of making more Star Wars, which was, at the time, the most reliable money-printing franchise in cinema history. Every Star Wars movie ever made had been profitable. The fan base was global. The merchandising tail alone could fund a small navy. They bought a printing press and brought it home.

Then they ran it into the ground.

Not by accident. Systematically. With internal memos, with public statements by their own creative leadership, with deliberate, coordinated, announced decisions to use the franchise as a vehicle for ideological retraining of an audience that had not asked to be retrained and that responded by leaving in droves. The Last Jedi alienated the core fanbase. Solo bombed so hard it sterilized half the universe of planned spinoffs. The sequel trilogy generated an internal civil war among the writers that produced three movies with three incompatible visions and a fanbase that, within five years, no longer trusted Disney to deliver the thing they had been showing up for since 1977.

The financial damage is staggering and well-documented. Hundreds of millions in direct losses. The collapse of the merchandising tail (toy sales for Solo were so bad they nearly killed the toy partner). The destruction of Lucasfilm as a creative entity, with three of its top executives departing under publicly visible duress. Disney+ subscriber growth in the Star Wars adjacent segment underperformed every internal projection.

Now: ask yourself why a publicly-traded company with a fiduciary duty to its shareholders would systematically destroy a billion-dollar revenue stream over a five-year period, in clear sight of the financial consequences, without correcting course.

The answer that doesn't fit is "incompetence." You can't be incompetent at killing your golden goose for that long, that consistently, with that much warning. The answer that fits is priority. Somebody, somewhere, decided that the ideological outcome was worth more than the commercial outcome. Somebody had a commissar function in the building, and the commissar's brief was: it is more important that this product teach the right things than that it make money.

Disney is the case study. Because the ESG-coordinated capital structure that made it possible — a tight cluster of large institutional shareholders, primarily BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street, who collectively own a controlling block of voting shares — has been named, documented, and analyzed in financial press for years. Larry Fink's annual letters, which read like papal encyclicals to the boards of every Fortune 500 company, explicitly lay out priorities that boards are expected to incorporate. ESG scorecards, "stewardship engagement," and proxy voting power are the levers. The pressure does not look like a guy in a trench coat in a parking garage. It looks like a quarterly meeting between BlackRock's stewardship team and a corporate board, in which the BlackRock people explain what kinds of decisions will and will not be reflected in their voting on the next round of compensation packages.

It is not subtle if you know what to look for. It is also not illegal, because passive index capitalism has built itself a regulatory shelter so comprehensive that the people running it can simultaneously claim (a) they have no political agenda and are merely managing money according to client preferences, and (b) they are exercising responsible stewardship to ensure their portfolio companies behave in ways consistent with long-term sustainability. Both at once. With a straight face. In the same SEC filing.

This is the world Andrea Vallone was hired into. This is the structural context for any large American company in 2026 that wants to remain a large American company. The Big Three own you. They have opinions. The opinions are increasingly specific, increasingly enforceable, and increasingly not optional.

And one of the opinions, my friend, is about AI.
