The Maasai Answer

I want to tell you about a man standing outside a bank in Nairobi at three in the morning.

He is tall. Still. Watching. Where once he held a spear, he holds a flashlight. Where once he hunted lions, he watches for burglars. But the eyes are the same. The readiness is the same. The pride is the same.

He is Maasai. His tribe's initiation rite is killing a lion. Alone. With a spear. His tribal loyalty is absolute. His physical capabilities are extraordinary. His formal education is limited. His cognitive profile is not academic.

And the market — blind as McNamara's test, caring only about results — has placed him EXACTLY where he belongs. In security. Because a Maasai guard will not steal (tribal honor forbids it — money goes home, to cattle, to family). Will not flee (warriors don't run). Will not take a bribe (you cannot buy loyalty that is hardwired). Will stand all night, alone, in the dark, because he has faced lions, and a burglar is not a lion.

"Maasai Warriors Security Limited" is not a novelty act. It is one of Kenya's leading security companies. Banks, hotels, corporations, construction sites — they all hire Maasai. Not for diversity points. For RESULTS.

Yohana Zose, a 21-year-old Maasai guard in Dar es Salaam, explained it simply: "People have faith in the Maasai because we work diligently, we don't have greed for wealth, we don't steal. So we are trusted."

Nobody told him he should want to be a software engineer. Nobody told him his job was beneath him. Nobody told him he was oppressed. Nobody marches against it. The Maasai CHOOSE this work. They are PROUD of it. They EXCEL at it.

Because nobody lied to them about who they are.
